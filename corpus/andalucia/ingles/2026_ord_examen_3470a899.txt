COMPREHENSION (4 points). READ THE TEXT AND ANSWER ALL THE QUESTIONS.
THE THEFT OF THE CENTURY

 1         Last October, as the sun rose over Paris and visitors began queueing through the Louvre Museum’s corridors, one of the most
 2     shocking thefts in recent memory took place. Within eight minutes, a gang of thieves sped away from the heart of the French
 3     capital with some of the nation’s most valuable treasures.
 4         According to French authorities, the gang that broke into the Apollo Gallery, just a short walk from the Mona Lisa, was clearly
 5     professional. They knew exactly what they were after. Parking a lorry equipped with an elevating platform like those used by
 6     removal companies, they lifted themselves to a first-floor balcony and used a disc cutter to gain entry to the gallery.
 7         The thieves did not target world-famous paintings that could never be displayed or sold. Instead, they went for items that could
 8     be converted into cash. They made straight for two display cases containing what remains of the French crown jewels, taking eight
 9     items worth an estimated €88 million, including diadems, necklaces, earrings, and brooches. During the escape, there was a
10     moment of carelessness: they dropped Empress Eugénie’s exquisite 19th-century crown.
11         In spite of their historical and cultural value, the jewels—officially described as priceless—can be broken up and sold piece by
12     piece. Even large, famous diamonds can be recut. The final price of the stolen jewels may be smaller, but it would still be
13     considerable.
14         Despite its 230-year history, there have been relatively few thefts at the Louvre, which houses thousands of artworks famous
15     around the world. This low number of thefts is largely due to the museum’s tight security. Reflecting on the incident, a gallery
16     attendant on duty said, “For us, it was heartbreaking to see the display cases broken.” And she added, “Never for a moment did
17     we think there was such a risk; nobody can be prepared for that.” She was right: the only real precedent was the notorious 1911
18     disappearance of Leonardo da Vinci’s La Joconde, better known as the Mona Lisa. The thief hid in a closet overnight, removed
19     the painting from its frame, wrapped it in his jacket, tucked it under his arm, and simply walked out. He turned out to be an Italian
20     nationalist who believed da Vinci’s masterpiece belonged in Italy. The painting was recovered three years later and returned to the
21     Louvre.
22         Since the October incident, security measures have been reinforced across France’s cultural institutions. The Louvre has
23     transferred some of its most precious jewels to the Bank of France following the theft, which has been generally regarded as a
24     source of embarrassment for the French government.
