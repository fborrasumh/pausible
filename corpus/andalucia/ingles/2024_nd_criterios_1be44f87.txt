TEXT 1: THE ETHICS OF TRUE CRIME KEY

 1            True crime TV shows have been so popular in the past few years that they have become the object of academic study.
 2      Professor of Media Ethics Whitney Phillips claims that the reason for the current success of this genre, as compared to novels and
 3      movies, is the reality factor: audiences enjoy feeling engaged in an actual mystery happening before them.
 4            Research has found that white women are the largest demographic group consuming true crime. The explanation is that, since
 5      white women have anxiety about potential threats, they turn to true crime to feel better prepared in case anything violent were to
 6      happen to them. The fact that the target audience is basically white women determines the profile of typical victims in true crime
 7      —white and female. After all, audiences prefer to watch cases they can relate to. Phillips argues that this produces the “missing
 8      white woman syndrome,” that is, the idea that there’s only one type of victim whose story matters. The media reinforce this with
 9      many stories and updates about white female victims, while stories about marginalized groups like Indigenous women are rarely
10      shown, if at all. Even white women who are missing or murdered receive little media attention when they’re sex workers, because
11      they’re perceived as less valuable than “innocent” white women.
12            Another harmful effect resulting from the boom of true crime is the dehumanization of victims when their tragedies become
13      marketable content. As one victim said, “My trauma’s not your entertainment. Awareness is a whole different story.”
14            Fortunately, there’s an alternative: ethical true crime, which avoids sensationalising the narrative and honours victims. Further
15      still, it seeks a way to help their case and alert to social inequalities.

TEXT 2: POMFRET, VERMONT: THE TOWN THAT WENT VIRAL KEY

 1          With a mere handful of businesses, Pomfret, located in Vermont (US), is generally a quiet place. But in autumn, as “leaf-
 2      peepers” from everywhere descend on the region’s hills to witness its multicolored leaves, all that changes.
 3          Ever since images of Sleepy Hollow Farm, a private property set on a rustic road, began going viral on social media a few
 4      years ago, locals say that things have gotten out of hand. A quick look on Instagram reveals thousands of images of the farm’s
 5      winding road lined by trees lit up in autumnal reds and pumpkin oranges. It’s no wonder then that this unlikely farm has become
 6      known as one of “the most photographed places in the state.”
 7          Unfortunately, social media influencers regularly climb over gates covered with “No Trespassing” signs, get their “city cars”
 8      stuck on the narrow country road, and leave bodily waste by the roadside. “It’s a hotel and amusement park —bring all your friends,”
 9      laughed out an Instagrammer with 20,000 followers.
10          “It’s a beautiful spot. It’s too bad that it’s been ruined for everybody,” said Deborah Goodwin, a local councilwoman. “Tour
11      buses are constantly unloading crowds of people out there. We can’t have this anymore.” In 2022, town officials finally voted to
12      close the roads leading to the farm to non-residents during the autumn season, which annoyed the travelers who had come hoping
13      to capture a perfect autumnal photo.
14          “We enjoy having tourists. It’s a big part of the Vermont economy, and we want people to enjoy its bucolic beauty and visit the
15      shops,” Goodwin says. Her call is for responsible tourism that enjoys Pomfret’s beauty without ruining what makes it special —its
16      peaceful charm.
