TEXT 1: A PIZZA FOR THE QUEEN

 1          Pizza Margherita, whose toppings resemble the colours of the Italian flag, red (tomato), white (mozzarella) and green (basil),
 2      has an appealing story behind its name. It was created by Raffaele Esposito, a well-known pizza maker from Naples who owned
 3      a tavern called Pizzeria di Pietro e Basta Cosí. Tomato-based pizza was then considered the food of the poor people of Naples
 4      and mainly sold by the street vendors. In his book Le Coricollo, French author Alexandre Dumas noted the popularity of the tomato-
 5      and-fish-based pizzas sold in the streets of Naples. People would eat watermelons in summer and pizza throughout the year.
 6          According to the legend, Esposito was invited to the royal palace to prepare a pizza for Queen Margherita of Savoy, who had
 7      travelled to Naples with King Umberto I. Esposito prepared three different pizzas, one of which used a combination of tomatoes,
 8      mozzarella cheese and basil to emulate the red, white and green of the Italian flag. The Queen found this pizza absolutely delicious
 9      and she is said to have sent a note of appreciation to the pizza maker, who then named the pizza after the Queen.
10          Although the authenticity of the story has been questioned by some people, it helped Neapolitan Pizza gain enduring popularity
11      and the presentation and fine taste of Esposito’s Pizza Margherita made it one of the most recognizable symbols of Italian food
12      culture in the world. Some of the old traditional pizzerias still exist in Naples, and a trip to this capital city of the Campania region
13      must certainly include a visit to its historic pizzerias for both pizza and history lovers.

TEXT 2: WILL THE MACHINES TAKE CONTROL?

 1          The first time I interviewed Eric Schmidt —twelve years ago when he was the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of Google— I had
 2     a simple question about the technology that is spying and monetizing all our movements, opinions, relationships and tastes. “Friend
 3     or enemy?” I asked. “We’re friends,” Schmidt replied coolly.
 4          Now that the former Google executive has a book out titled The Age of Artificial Intelligence, I made him the same question
 5     about Artificial Intelligence (AI), “Friend or enemy?”. “AI is imprecise, and it can be unreliable as a partner,” he replied. “It’s dynamic
 6     since it’s changing all the time. It’s emergent and does things you don’t expect. And, most importantly, it’s capable of learning.”
 7          “It will be everywhere,” continues Schmidt. “How may a child feel about having an AI programmed robot? What does AI war
 8     look like? Does AI perceive aspects of reality that we don’t? Is it possible that AI will see things that humans cannot comprehend?”
 9          Some scientists believe that when we build AI without a kill bottom for it, we are creating a demon —with luck, humans could
10     end up as the family pets. On this point, Schmidt disagrees with those people who consider that by developing AI, eventually, you’ll
11     finish up with a master robot that’s 1,000 times smarter than the humans. “My answer is different. I think that these AI systems are
12     going to think, not like humans, but they’re going to be very smart, and that we’re going to have to coexist.”
13          I asked, “You don’t think Siri and Alexa are going to kill us one night?”. “No,” he replied. “But they might become your child’s
14     best friend.”
