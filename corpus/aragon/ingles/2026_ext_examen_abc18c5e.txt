                             LEARNING LANGUAGES TWO MINUTES EACH DAY

   Duolingo is the most frequently downloaded education app in the world. It was founded by the Guatemalan
   computer scientist Luis von Ahn, known for creating the test CAPTCHA, designed to tell computers and humans
   apart. In 2009, shortly after developing the system called reCAPTCHA, von Ahn sold it to Google for a sum that
   would ensure neither he nor his future children would ever need to work.

   Instead of retiring, he set up a new project, Duolingo, whose popularity spiked dramatically in March 2020, when
   COVID-19 shut hundreds of millions of people in their homes. But attracting people is one thing, and getting them
   to stay is another—this is Duolingo’s core business. When you begin a course on the app, you are greeted by the
   company’s mascot, a green owl named Duo. Then, cartoon characters speak sentences to you and prompt you
   to translate them. Users are reminded to finish at least one lesson each day. They earn points and trophies, and
   move through increasingly challenging levels.

   In order to engage learners’ brains in different ways, Duolingo deliberately limits the kind of explicit grammar
   instruction we might associate with an old-fashioned foreign-language class. It pushes learners to use the building
   blocks of a language, and to understand, through that experience, how they fit together. The lessons do not
   exceed, on average, two minutes, although that length has been decreasing. “Already we’re a little worried that
   younger generations actually expect thirty-second activities, not a two-minute thing”, von Ahn said.

  Another key element is the number of user repetitions, which generates an enormous amount of data. While the
  app teaches users, users are simultaneously teaching the app to be a better instructor. Duolingo is now using
  GPT-4, a large language model created by the artificial-intelligence company OpenAI. It has been trained on far
  more data than previous models.

  Von Ahn believes that artificial intelligence would eventually make computers better teachers than people. He
  considers this a hopeful development, since more people have access to smartphones than to high-quality
  education. “I really want people to be able to learn for free”, he said.

  Surprisingly, a recent study reveals that most teachers do not oppose Duolingo. Instead, they see it as
  supplemental to the kind of deep immersion that language learning requires. Despite von Ahn’s predictions, they
  find it highly unlikely that apps will replace human instructors in the upcoming future.
                                        (Adapted from “How much can Duolingo teach us?”, The New Yorker)

3.1. Write an opinion essay on the following statement: “In the 21st century, human teachers are not essential
anymore”.
  In the twenty-first century, technology has transformed education in fundamental ways. Online
  platforms, educational apps and artificial intelligence have significantly increased learner
  independence, to the point that some people doubt that human teachers are still essential. I find this
  view problematic.
  On the one hand, digital tools offer clear advantages. For example, information can be accessed
  instantly so that students can learn at their own pace. Moreover, education is more accessible to
  those who cannot attend in-person classes.
  However, what human teachers provide can never be fully replaced by technology. Good teachers
  contribute to students’ motivation, consider individual needs, and provide emotional support. In
  addition, teachers are role models, helping students develop key personal, academic, and
  professional skills.
  In conclusion, despite the usefulness of technology, modern education cannot be understood
  without human teachers. Instead, the best results will be achieved if technology and teachers work
  together.
