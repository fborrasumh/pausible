Option 1.
                                         Screen time in Japan
 1 Amid growing concerns about excessive screen time, the city council of Toyoake in Japan is
   asking its residents to limit their recreational use of smartphones and tablets to no more than
   two hours per day. Official guidelines on the use of digital devices came into municipal law in
   October 2025. The mayor of Toyoake explained: “Digital technologies are wonderful, but it is
   important to limit the time we spend on them for non-work or non-educational purposes. We
   also need to think about our youth. Many children and teenagers have their own smartphones
   and bedrooms. With nothing else to do, they spend hours staring at screens. Day turns to
   night, their sleep suffers, and the cycle repeats.” The primary purpose of the law is to ensure
   sufficient sleeping hours among children and adolescents. For children aged 6 to 12, the
   guidelines recommend switching off phones and tablets by 9:00 pm to ensure that sleep is
   not disturbed. Older children, and even adults, are encouraged to switch off by 10:00 pm.
 2 The Toyoake city council first proposed the law in August and immediately received criticism
   from residents. Local councillor Mariko Fujie argued: “Many residents believe this is total
   nonsense. For my part, I feel a strong resistance to regulating people's free time with a law.”
   Councillor Fujie also published an open letter in the local newspaper arguing that the
   guidelines were not based on scientific evidence. However, several studies carried out in Asia
   have confirmed the negative impact of screen time on wellbeing. For example, one study of
   teenagers in China found that sleep patterns, social relationships, and academic performance
   were all negatively affected by the number of hours participants spent on their devices.

 3 In reality, the law is more symbolic than enforced. The Toyoake city council will not track
   residents’ phone usage, and it will not issue fines to those who exceed the recommended
   two-hour limit. Instead, the guidelines are more about encouraging users to carefully consider
   their screen time. Although the measure was controversial at first, many residents have
   responded positively to it. University student Shoki Moriyama has reduced his screen time
   from eight hours to just under two, admitting that although it had been a struggle, it was worth
   it: “I now use that extra time to study or go to the gym.” Local resident Akari Saito agrees: “I
   found it especially hard to put my phone away during the train to school or while I was walking,
   but I can see that reducing my phone use will allow me to spend my day more meaningfully.”

Option 2.
                                         Overtourism
 1 Tourism is booming. In 2024, there were 1.5 billion holidaymakers worldwide, setting the
   second-highest ever record after 2019. Overtourism refers to the excessive influx of
   holidaymakers to a destination. The UN World Tourism Organisation defines the phenomenon
   as follows: “It is the point at which locals or visitors feel tourism has negatively impacted the
   quality of life in a region to an unacceptable degree.” This certainly applies to the Canary
   Islands. Home to 2.2 million residents, the islands hosted 15.2 million visitors last year, and a
   new record is expected for 2026. Tourism accounts for more than one-third of the Canary
   Islands' economy, but local community groups argue that the tourism industry is transforming
   the islands in ways that do not serve local needs.

 2 Overtourism stretches infrastructure and local resources to their limit. Noise, littering, and
   traffic jams are just a few of the unpleasant aspects of surges in visitors. Landscapes are often
   altered, disrupting the local flora and fauna. Overtourism also threatens local water resources,
   particularly in places like the Canary Islands, where supplies are limited, and holidaymakers
   generally consume more water than locals. Moreover, tourism today accounts for 10% of
   global greenhouse gas emissions, with air travel mainly to blame for its growing climate impact.
   Short trips with low-cost airlines are increasingly popular in Europe and are particularly
   damaging, as the largest proportion of tourists’ carbon emissions comes from the flights to and
   from their destinations.
 3 They say the presence of nearby airports, particularly those served by low-cost airlines,
   encourages overtourism. Mallorca, for example, would perhaps not be so popular if people
   could only travel there by ship. However, cruise ships are also criticised for aggravating the
   problem. Cruise ships carry large numbers of passengers, who usually go ashore for only a
   few hours, putting pressure on local infrastructure and contributing very little to the local
   economy, as everything they need is on board.
 4 But can overtourism be prevented? Some regions are trying to limit the number of visitors they
 . receive. Tenerife is restricting access to the summit of the Pico del Teide volcano to 300 visitors
   per day, and Thailand has gone one step further; to help local ecosystems recover, the Thai
   government has closed the popular Similan and Surin Islands entirely to the public this year
   until autumn 2026. Restrictions aside, there are also places that reward climate-friendly
   travellers. Last year, Copenhagen city council launched CopenPay, a campaign that rewards
   visitors with bonuses such as reduced admission prices to local attractions or a free cup of
   coffee when they travel to the city by train rather than by aeroplane.
