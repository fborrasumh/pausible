                                            Greenwashing
 1     Being green is big business, with one study showing that 66% of consumers worldwide are
       willing to pay more for products they believe to be socially and environmentally responsible.
       However, shoppers should exercise caution when shopping sustainably, as much of the
       language companies use to sell their products is not what it seems. The practice of
       greenwashing involves companies making misleading or exaggerated environmental
       statements about their products in order to gain a competitive advantage over similar items
       on supermarket shelves. "Companies are under a lot of pressure to become eco-friendly
       because consumers are looking for this," explains eco-influencer Mandy Spooner, "so they
       present a 'greener' image of themselves through advertising and packaging."
 2     Some of the marketing tricks companies use include natural-looking imagery and vague
       terminology. For example, terms like biodegradable and compostable are often more
       complicated than they appear, as many products cannot be processed in a household
       compost bin and instead need specific commercial conditions to break down. Another strong
       example of a commonly greenwashed product is bottled water. Despite being marketed as
       clean and pure, the water industry drains local natural resources and uses vast quantities of
       petrol, shipping their "product" thousands of kilometres around the world. It is also a
       significant contributor to plastic waste. Just because a plastic water bottle is recyclable does
       not mean it will be.
 3     A significant fallout of greenwashing is that it can erode consumer confidence, as Spooner
       explains: "When companies make sustainability claims that turn out to be false, they not only
       damage their own reputation but also brands genuinely committed to doing the right thing."

Option 2.
                                                  BookTok
 1       BookTok is a TikTok subcommunity where people make content about the literature they
         love. Videos range from book reviews and reading recommendations to “book hauls” and
         bookshelf setups. What started as a small community of avid readers has now become
         one of the most popular corners of the social media platform, with #BookTok gaining 92.7
         billion views and some posts up to 100,000 likes.
 2       Publishing industry insiders say that BookTok has led to an unprecedented boost in book
         sales. Kristen McLean is the executive director of NPD Bookscan, an industry data provider
         that tracks book sales. She says that, along with the COVID-19 pandemic, BookTok has
         definitely been a factor in the recent boom in sales. McLean first noticed the platform’s
         influence in 2020, when it appeared to drive up sales in the young adult fiction genre before
         expanding into adult fiction and non-fiction.
 3       One example of the “BookTok effect” is the massive success of author Colleen Hoover.
         Before BookTok became popular in 2020, Hoover had sold only 200,000 copies of her
         books. In August 2022, she sold 2.3 million copies, six of which entered the New York
         Times’ fiction bestseller list. Another example is the number of older books becoming
         popular years after their initial release. Taylor Jenkins Reid's The Seven Husbands of
         Evelyn Hugo and Madeline Miller's The Song of Achilles went viral years after their
         publication, and both landed on bestseller lists many years later.
 4       Many publishers and book retailers are keen to cash in on the BookTok trend. Some
         publishers hire younger employees who are familiar with the platform and can help create
         marketing campaigns that resonate with the BookTok community. Other publishers work
         directly with influencers, sending them book copies prior to release and paying them to
         promote the new titles. However, these strategies do not always work, as BookTok
         influencer Kendra Keeter explains: “A lot of sponsored content doesn’t do well because it
         just isn’t authentic. How can you trust someone’s book recommendation if you know they
         were paid to post?” BookTok is also influencing how retailers do business, with many
         dedicating spaces to “BookTok favourites” in online and physical shops. “BookTok has had
         an immense impact on sales,” says James Daunt from Barnes & Noble Books, “It’s bringing
         energy into our shops, with people of all ages coming together around a shared love for
         reading.”
