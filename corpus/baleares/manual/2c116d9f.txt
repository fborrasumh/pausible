Hollywood’s ‘nepo babies’ should acknowledge their privilege

Hollywood is full of “nepo babies”. It may sound like some sort of children’s craze but the reality is

far more unseemly. The term, of course, refers to the children of celebrities who go on to pursue

careers in show business. The Maya Hawkes, Lily Rose Depps and Jaden Smiths of the world. This is

hardly a new development; from Judy Garland and Liza Minelli to the Bridges and the Fondas,

Hollywood has always loved a dynasty. But now, it seems, more than ever, hordes of celebrity children

are surfing to fame and have their famous parents to thank for their success. And people are not

having it.

On Monday, the model and musician Lourdes Leon – Madonna’s eldest daughter – gave an

interview with   The Cut   in which she discussed her own “nepo baby” status. “I want to feel like I

deserve things and not just like I’ve been given things”, she said. “And, yes, there’s undeniable

privilege that I’d be stupid to not realise... Nepotism babies are pretty awful usually, and my mom

and my father raised me to be so much smarter than that”. The truth is, Hollywood’s many star-

children have a responsibility to fully acknowledge their own privilege. Nepotism must be brought to

light if it is to be confronted.

In Hollywood, the framework for nepotism is harder to remove than in other industries. For a

mid- or low-budget production, hiring the child of a famous star guarantees free and immediate

publicity, intrigue and name recognition, without the fees that usually come with it. Social media has

streamlined the process, too. Instagram has catapulted many a nepotism baby into the limelight from

a young age; some arrive at the doors of Hollywood having already millions of followers.

Then there is the genetic factor. This comes in terms of innate talent, I suppose. But on a

more superficial level, it boils down to appearances. Call it what you will – sex appeal, telegenicism…

Whether we are talking movies or record labels, show business favours the good-looking. When two

telegenic actors have a child, odds are that their child will grow up to be just as telegenic. Combine

that with the dietary and fitness privileges of a wealthy upbringing, and you are playing with some

seriously loaded dice. Perversely, there are also ways in which the entertainment industry is, in the

long run, more meritocratic than most other spheres of work. If Miley Cyrus could not sing, she would

not be performing at the Super Bowl. If Nicolas Cage could not open a movie, it would not have been

long before studios stopped casting him. Show business is a popularity contest.

So what, then, is the solution here? Hollywood nepotism, and nepotism in general, feed into

wider social divisions that only a large-scale redistribution of wealth could hope to address. For now,

just talking about it may have to be enough. The first step has always been admitting there is a

problem.

Jenna Ortega knows what Wednesday Addams wants

Jenna   Ortega   plays   the   title   character   in   the   new   Netflix   murder   mystery   series

“Wednesday”, based on the pigtailed and pessimistic Addams Family character, which

debuted — when else? — on Wednesday. But for Ortega, the interest lies in the character’s

deeper layers. “My Wednesday has this concealed confidence”, Ortega said. “She’s on a

mission, and she won’t let anyone get in her way”.

Unlike the “Addams Family” films of the early 1990s, in which Christina Ricci played

a 10-year-old Wednesday Addams, the new eight-episode series, set in the present day,

features Ortega as a 16-year-old version of the character, who is sent to a boarding school

for outcasts after an incident gets her expelled from her public high school. Even in a school

populated by vampires and werewolves — her parents, Morticia and Gomez met there as

students — Wednesday is a freak among freaks, a frequent subject of gossip because of

her, and her family’s bloody past. But Wednesday is also the latest in a long line of teen

roles for Ortega, a former Disney star who was in some ways anxious to move on. “I

definitely had hesitations about doing it”, she said. Still, it is not every role that requires

you to immerse yourself in learning a half-dozen new skills, including fencing, canoeing,

archery, German and twice-weekly cello lessons.

At 20, Ortega has already been performing in front of cameras for half her life. When

the creators   of   “Wednesday”,   Al   Gough   and   Miles   Millar,   began   developing   their lead

character, they did not want to follow the typical shy-to-confident arc of teenage female

protagonists.   “Wednesday   starts   strong   and   stays   that   way”,   Millar   said.   “She’s

unapologetic, fearless, smart, weird — it’s very rare to see a female teen character who’s

that sure of herself”. But bringing their collective vision to fruition was going to require the

perfect Wednesday. They auditioned hundreds of actresses, but Ortega brought to the

character the empathy they were looking for.

Burton, the director of classic horror-fantasy films like “Beetlejuice” and “Edward

Scissorhands”, got to work putting his stamp on the series, which was filmed in Romania

over eight months in 2021 and 2022. Burton was involved in even the smallest details,

styling Ortega’s bangs himself with a comb before a hairdresser sprayed them into place

and instructing Ortega on how Wednesday should stand. But he was also remarkably hands-

off when it came to other elements, Ortega said, allowing her the freedom to arrive at her

own interpretation of the character.

Is   she   worried   she   will   be   categorised   as   a   horror   queen,   especially   after

“Wednesday”? “A little”, she said. But she still finds it hard to turn down a horror script.

“Horror is very therapeutic”, she said. “You’re screaming bloody murder — you don’t get

the chance to do that in your everyday life — so it’s a way to excavate all the unnecessary,

repressed stress”.
