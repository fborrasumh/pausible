Read the following text:
For many years, British dance music was mostly controlled by men. Female DJs were few, and often
chosen for their looks rather than their musical talent. Now, things have changed a lot. Women are not
only taking part but also leading the genre. They headline big festivals, win important prizes, and
influence the sound of the scene.
In the 1990s and 2000s, it was rare to see a woman behind the DJ decks. A female DJ remembers that
club promoters often booked only one woman per event. Many did not take female DJs seriously. Today,
it is common to see event posters with equal numbers of men and women—or even female-led line-
ups. In some cases, women are the majority of performers.
Part of this change started with small projects. Some groups began offering training, mentoring, and
networking to women and non-binary DJs. These collectives helped break the “boys’ club” culture that
had been common in the DJ world. Social media and streaming platforms also played a big role. These
tools allow artists to share their music and connect with fans directly—without needing approval from
male-run record labels or promoters.
Wider social changes also had an impact. The MeToo movement increased awareness of sexism in the
music industry. This led to stronger calls for safe and inclusive spaces in clubs and festivals. Many venues
now have diversity policies and safer-space rules. These steps have made it easier for women to feel
welcome and respected in the dance music community.
Some recent successes have inspired others to follow. In 2022, a dance track by a female DJ reached
number one in the UK singles chart. It was the first dance track by a female DJ to do this in more than
20 years. Now, some female DJs are famous for their sets, and they play at major festivals. Others have
won important music awards for their modern tracks.
However, there are still challenges. Female DJs sometimes face online abuse and are judged more
harshly than male DJs for mistakes. Representation is not equal across all styles—women are more
visible in house and disco, but still rare in drum’n’bass and techno.
Even with these difficulties, the progress is clear. Women are now producing music, managing record
labels, and organising major music events. As one of them says: “This wouldn’t have happened 20 years
ago. We’ve gone from being tokens to being a movement.”
