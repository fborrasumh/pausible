My seven-month-old daughter was lying on her play mat, holding her tiny feet in

to finish the sentence in English. This mix of simple Mandarin, English words and
my strong Australian accent is what I am passing on to her. When I read her
Chinese baby books, I often don’t know many of the words. It feels like we are

It’s strange that I am now trying to teach her Mandarin, because, when I was
young, I tried hard to hide my Chinese background. While growing up in Australia
in the 1980s and 90s, I spoke loudly in a clear Australian accent so that no one
would think I was “too Chinese”. At that time, it felt safer to distance myself from
my culture. My father had arrived from Malaysia in 1973, just as Australia was
moving away from the White Australia rule. He believed that fitting into
Anglo-Australian culture was necessary.

My parents worked hard and sent my siblings and me to good private schools.
There, I met girls who seemed to represent the “ideal” Australian image: tall,
confident country girls. I didn’t realise that my own story—children of immigrants
working for a better future—was also becoming part of Australia’s identity. My
parents tried to raise me bilingual. When I was about ten, they sent me to
Saturday Chinese school. But I felt embarrassed because younger children who
spoke Chinese at home were far ahead of me. I soon gave up.
