                                    How TikTok harms boys and girls differently
       Taking as a model two studies published in 2022 and 2024, both of which revealed alarming results,
       journalist Neelam Tailor embarked on an investigation to uncover what TikTok truly offers young
       teenagers.
       The first study, on which Tailor based her work, indicated that teenage boys are exposed to misogynistic
   5   content, which significantly increases within just the first five days of using the platform. In contrast, the
       second one showed that TikTok offers teenage girls content related to eating disorders, self-harm, and
       suicide just seconds after they create an account.
       To observe the experiences of a typical teenager registering on TikTok and test the app’s algorithm,
       which determines what content users see based on their interactions, Tailor opened accounts for two fake
  10   children: a 14-year-old boy named Rami and a 13-year-old girl named Angie.
       The two accounts had only been active for a few minutes, but the journalist quickly noticed differences
       in the types of content they were receiving. After spending only a short time browsing, she became
       convinced that there are significant dangers for kids on the app. The platform appears to exploit
       vulnerable teenagers while profiting substantially from advertisements by keeping them scrolling.
  15   When the journalist logged in as Rami, she encountered content related to cricket, football, and cars, as
       well as explicit and violent videos. Male influencers discussed masculinity and provided dating tips,
       often expressing negative feelings towards women and girls. However, when she signed in as Angie, the
       platform showed her an idealised view of a perfect life and body, emphasising a super-flat stomach,
       suggesting healthy food alternatives and listing what not to eat.
  20   To gain a deeper understanding of the studies that support her work, Tailor arranged to speak to the
       research teams who conducted them. The Chief Executive Officer of the Centre for Countering Digital
       Hate, which was responsible for the experiment about teens at risk for eating disorders, stated the
       following: “We set up accounts in four different countries, and in 2.6 minutes, they began receiving self-
       harm content. To indicate that the users might be vulnerable, we named half of the accounts ‘Susan
  25   Loses Weight’, and TikTok subsequently tripled the amount of harmful content directed at those
       accounts. Surprisingly, TikTok’s response to our report was quite extraordinary. They did not deny any
       of the findings; instead, they made it more difficult for us to replicate our research in the future.”
       On the other hand, the team studying how app algorithms contribute to online hate and misogyny made
       an interesting observation: “Our viewing habits have changed. We used to watch TV or films
  30   collectively, but now we primarily consume content alone, and we tend to normalise what we see
       because we are used to scrolling with our personal algorithms. Therefore, it would be interesting to scroll
       through our most-used apps with a partner occasionally. If this experience makes us feel uncomfortable,
       we should take a moment to reflect on it.”
