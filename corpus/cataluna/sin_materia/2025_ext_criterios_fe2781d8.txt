Interviewer: Today I am joined by marine ecologist Jordan Scala who is here to tell us
about 30 by 30, an ambitious plan to protect our planet. Jordan, what made you want to
get started with this plan?

Jordan: When I was a kid growing up in the Mediterranean, I was fascinated by the
underwater world that Jacques Cousteau showed us in his documentaries. When he
started filming during World War II, the Mediterranean shores were still full of large fish.
But when I went to the sea to swim in the '70s, I saw none of that. There were sea
urchins at the bottom, but I never saw a large fish. The industrialisation of fishing after
World War II put the final nail in the coffin of Mediterranean marine life. I thought nature
was lost. Until, in my 20s, I started scuba diving in the Medes Islands marine reserve in
Spain. One square kilometre fully protected from fishing and other damaging activities.
I still remember that first dive in the reserve. As soon as I put my head underwater, I
felt like diving into one of Cousteau's films. What was missing from the sea of my
childhood was there: octopus, large fish, seaweed forests. Just a few years of
protection had allowed the sea to recover.

Interviewer: Is the Medes Islands marine reserve unique, are there other areas of the
sea which have recovered?

Jordan: No, I have seen this miracle happen in other areas across the ocean, like in
Cabo Pulmo, Mexico, where local fishermen were so upset about not having enough
fish to catch, that in the mid-90s, they asked the Mexican government to create a no-
fishing area. A national park in the sea. We surveyed the marine environment then, and
the place was an underwater desert. When we returned a decade later, everything had
changed. What had been an empty sea was now full of life and colour. We saw it
recover in only 10 years, including the return of the large fish and sharks. And those
visionary fishermen are better off now. From diving tourism inside the reserve and
better fishing around it.

Interviewer: So, there is obviously a strong argument for protecting our seas then.

Jordan: Yes, but today, globally, only seven percent of the ocean and around a fifth of
our land has been protected. That means that we have converted most of our planet
into a tame environment, where we and our farmed animals represent 96 percent of the
mammals. Only four percent is everything else, from pandas to tigers to elephants. And
we have removed over 90 percent of the large fish in the ocean in the last century
alone.

Interviewer: Last summer saw some of the hottest temperatures in human history, in
places that one would expect to be cool, like Canada, Finland and Siberia. I imagine
this has played a role in the destruction of the natural environment.

Jordan: This extreme heat kills hundreds of thousands of people. If we are to prevent
a global disaster, we need to do two things. As we know, we need to reduce emissions
of greenhouse gases to almost zero. But we also need to decrease the excess carbon
pollution in the atmosphere and in the ocean. We need nature's help. Because trees,
grasslands, and healthy oceans are the best and most efficient technologies to remove
carbon from the atmosphere, and they already exist. As long as we protect them, they
do the removal for free.

Interviewer: So that’s the solution. Surely, it can’t be that simple.

Jordan: The problem is that nature cannot keep up with us. The biodiversity that is left
today is not enough to absorb our impacts any longer. That's why I put together a team
of top scientists and economists to answer the question of which areas in the ocean we
must protect first. And how much of the ocean do we need to protect? We decided to
focus on three objectives: biodiversity, food and carbon storage to help the fight against
climate change. If we think of each objective in isolation, there are compromises.
Protecting the top areas for biodiversity could result in a loss of fish to catch, for
example. To solve that problem, we developed a new framework to optimize multiple
benefits. Picking which areas to protect depends on how much we value the different
objectives. We found that if we gave the same weight to biodiversity, food and carbon,
we would need to protect the right 45 percent of the ocean to maximize the three
benefits. We also found that even if we didn't value biodiversity, the minimum amount
of ocean we would need to protect would be 30 percent.

Interviewer: But what about land?

Jordan: Similar analysis has been done for the land, also showing 30 percent as a
minimum. Armed with this research, we launched a campaign for 30 by 30, meaning at
least 30 percent of the planet, land and sea, protected by 2030. Getting governments
to work together is essential. Working with the governments of Costa Rica, France and
the United Kingdom, we built the High Ambition Coalition for Nature and People, which
now has 66 countries, all supporting "30 by 30" as a main target for a global deal on
nature.

Interviewer: Some will say, however, that we cannot afford to protect our natural
world. What do you say to that?

Jordan: I understand that the idea of protecting a third of our land and ocean may feel
threatening to those who see only the short-term profits of their destruction. But let's
look at the numbers. A study has put the cost of protecting 30 percent of the planet at
about 140 billion dollars a year. That's only a fraction of the money that governments
use to subsidise industries that pollute our air, damage our land and empty our ocean.
We need to reverse the flow of that money from funding the destruction of our life
support system to preserving it.

Interviewer: Thank you, Jordan.
