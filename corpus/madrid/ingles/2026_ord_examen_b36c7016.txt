                                      Musicians Feel Pain Differently

   It is well known that learning to play an instrument gives advantages beyond music. Research
   shows it is a great activity for the brain. It can improve coordination, speech and memory. It can
   even help to keep our brains younger. Making music is more than a creative skill, it is also a mental
   exercise that strengthens how we think and feel.

   After working with musicians for years, we saw how they kept practising despite feeling pain from
   repeated movements. That made us wonder: if musical training can reshape the brain, can it also
   change how musicians feel pain? Pain activates many reactions in our body and brain. For
   example, touching something hot makes you move your hand immediately. Pain also changes
   brain activity. Usually, it reduces activity in the area of the brain that controls movement. This helps
   you stop using an injured body part too much and protects you in the short term. However, if pain
   continues, problems can appear. For example, if you injure your ankle and avoid using it for weeks,
   mobility decreases and brain activity is disrupted. This can increase pain.

   Research also shows that long-lasting pain can make the brain’s “body map” smaller. This map
   helps the muscles know when and how to move. When the map gets smaller, the pain can feel
   stronger. However, not everyone reacts the same way. Some people tolerate pain better than
   others. In our study, we wanted to find out if musical training changes how musicians deal with
   pain. We created temporary hand pain in musicians and non-musicians. To do this, we used a
   special substance called “nerve growth factor”. This substance is safe but it makes muscles ache
   for days.

   We then measured brain activity by sending small magnetic signals into the brain to see how it
   controls the hand. We made hand maps before the injection, two days later, and eight days later.
   The results were clear. Even before feeling pain, musicians had more detailed hand maps. The
   more hours they practised, the better these maps were. After feeling pain, musicians reported less
   discomfort. In non-musicians, the brain map became smaller after only two days. In musicians, the
   maps stayed the same. The more they had trained, the less pain they felt.

   This was a small study with only 40 people, but findings show that musicians’ brains respond
   differently to pain. Their training seems to protect them.

   Adapted from Ana Zamorano’s “Neuroscience Finds Musicians Feel Pain Differently from the Rest of Us.”
   The Conversation, 24 Sep. 2025. <https://theconversation.com/neuroscience-finds-musicians-feel-pain-
   diﬀerently-from-the-rest-of-us-265815>
